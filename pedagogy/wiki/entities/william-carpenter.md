# William Carpenter

**Type:** person (English physician, physiologist, and naturalist, 1813–1885)

## Summary

The Victorian physiologist whose book _Principles of Mental Physiology_ (1874) — referred to by [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] simply as _Mental Physiology_ — supplied the **physiological foundation of Mason's doctrine of [[concepts/habit-formation|habit]]**. Mason names Carpenter's work in the Preface to the Fourth Edition of _Home Education_ as the source she is "indebted" to for the habit chapters, and in Part III calls it "perhaps the first which gave me the clue I was in search of."

## Why Mason Relies on Him

- **The body forms itself to its modes of action.** Carpenter's school held that growing tissue adapts its structure to the action habitually required of it — walking, writing, and dancing all become "perfectly easy and natural" because the muscles "grow to" the action.
- **The brain is no exception.** Mason quotes Carpenter directly: "the cerebrum... grows to the modes of thought in which it is habitually exercised," and any frequently repeated "sequence of mental action... tends to perpetuate itself." This is the physiological half of [[concepts/habit-formation|habit formation]] — the picture of a habit as a "rut" worn in the nervous substance.
- **Incessant regeneration.** Carpenter's idea that brain tissue is constantly renewed, and that the new tissue "grows to" whatever habit of thought is in force, gives Mason her claim that the parent literally shapes "the actual conformation of the child's brain."
- **Regularity from infancy.** Mason quotes Carpenter on infant training: "_Regularity_ should begin even with infant life, as to times of feeding, repose, etc. The _bodily_ habit thus formed greatly helps to shape the _mental_ habit at a later period."

## Connections

- The intellectual taproot of [[concepts/habit-formation|habit formation]] — Mason's most distinctive practical doctrine.
- Mason pairs Carpenter with a quoted passage from **Huxley** on "artificial reflex actions" acquired by drill (the discharged soldier who drops his dinner at the word "Attention!").

> [!note] Carpenter's physiology is Victorian associationist science. Mason's _behavioural_ conclusions (habits are durable, automatic, and best changed by substitution) have largely held up; the specific _mechanism_ she takes from him — new brain tissue "growing to" a habit — is a period theory and should be read as such, not as current neuroscience.
