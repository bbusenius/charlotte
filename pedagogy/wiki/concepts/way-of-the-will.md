# Way of the Will

## Definition

[[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]]'s account of volition and self-command — Principles 16–17 of the [[concepts/20-principles-of-education|20 Principles]] and the subject of Chapter VIII of [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|_Philosophy of Education_]]. The "Way of the Will" is, for Mason, one of the two great guides to the moral and intellectual life (the other being the Way of the Reason).

## Core Ideas

- **The will is not the same as desire, emotion, or habit.** Most of what is popularly called "strong will" Mason regards as strong passion, or strong habit, or obstinacy — not will proper.
- **Will is the power of choice** — specifically, the power to choose what the mind will attend to.
- **Direct assault on temptation fails.** Effort of will turned straight on to an unwanted desire generally intensifies it. (A hundred years before the psychological literature on ironic rebound effects, Mason makes the observation in plain English.)
- **The remedy is diversion.** Will is exercised not by pressing harder against the desire but by turning the attention firmly to something else — a different subject, a different task. Mason calls this the **rebound** or **diversion** of the will. When attention returns to the original question, choice can be made freshly and cleanly.
- **Will is trained like a habit.** Children should be given small, daily opportunities to exercise choice — and to practise the act of turning attention away from what tempts or distresses them.
- **Will requires right ideas to choose from.** This is where [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]] interlocks with the Way of the Reason (Chapter IX): reason will demonstrate whatever premise the will accepts, so the child must be fed sound initial ideas. Mason's long critique of Marx's _Manifesto_ is her illustration that a forceful will admitting a faulty premise produces a cogent-looking argument for disastrous action.

## In Practice

- [[concepts/habit-formation|Habits]] are laid down early — of [[concepts/habit-of-attention|attention]], obedience, truthfulness — so that the will is not spent daily on routine choices. (Mason warns, conversely, that a bad habit can "frustrate the will": the drunkard wills with what strength he has, but habit overpowers it.)
- When a child is upset, angry, obsessed, or tempted, the adult helps by redirecting attention to a new and genuinely interesting object (a book, an errand, a piece of work), not by arguing down the feeling.
- Moral teaching is mostly indirect: through great lives in [[concepts/living-books|living books]] — Plutarch, the Gospels, Scott, Shakespeare — rather than hortatory exhortation, which Mason thinks bores children and often backfires.
- The "Way of the Will" is taught explicitly to older pupils through Mason's own text _Ourselves, our Souls and Bodies_, used across the [[entities/parents-union-school|PUS]].

## Variants & Related Concepts

- **Way of the Reason** (Chapter IX) — the partner concept: reason serves whatever premises the will entertains.
- [[concepts/children-are-born-persons|Children are Born Persons]] — the child already has a will; adults do not install one.
- [[concepts/20-principles-of-education|20 Principles]] — Principles 16–19.
- [[concepts/education-is-atmosphere-discipline-life|Education is an Atmosphere, a Discipline, a Life]] — "discipline" (habit) prepares the ground on which the will can operate freely.

## Key Proponents / Origins

- [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] — the developed pedagogical form is hers, and an early one: the doctrine is fully worked out in [[sources/home-education|_Home Education_]] (1906, from 1885 lectures). She draws on Christian moral tradition and on Coleridge (on whom she often quotes approvingly).

## Mentioned In

- [[sources/home-education|Home Education]] — Volume I (1906), the wiki's earliest source for the doctrine. Part VI, Chapter I ("The Will") frames the will as the executive of "the kingdom of Mansoul," distinguishes it from "wilfulness" (which is the _want_ of will, not its exercise), and gives the operating mechanism — the will redirects the mind by incentive, diversion, and change of thought. Mason puts it in words a child can use: "Are you cross? Change your thoughts." Part VI continues into Chapter II ("The Conscience" — "I am, I ought, I can, I will," and the instructed versus the uninstructed conscience) and Chapter III ("The Divine Life in the Child"), which together carry Mason's account of moral and spiritual formation.
- [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|Towards a Philosophy of Education — Book I]] — Chapter VIII ("The Way of the Will") and Principles 16–17; Chapter IX on the Way of the Reason picks up the partner doctrine.
- [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-2|Towards a Philosophy of Education — Book II]] — Book II sharpens the "reason-as-servant-of-the-will" theme into a political warning: whole nations act on the demonstrations their reason makes from whatever premises their will accepts, so the business of education is to supply sound initial ideas to every citizen.
