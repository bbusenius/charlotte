# Method versus System

## Definition

A foundational distinction [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] draws in the opening chapter of _Home Education_. A **method** is "a way to an end, and step-by-step progress in that way" — it begins from a clear idea of the end to be reached and follows natural law toward it. A **system** is the observance of fixed rules until a habit of doing certain things is confirmed. Mason holds that education must be conducted as a method; reduced to a system, it "is mischievous, as producing only mechanical action instead of the vital growth and movement of a living being."

## Core Ideas

- **A method follows natural law.** Method is "natural; easy, yielding, unobtrusive, simple as the ways of Nature herself; yet, watchful, careful, all-pervading, all-compelling." A parent who genuinely _sees his way_ "will make use of every circumstance of the child's life almost without intention on his own part" — Mason's image is the sun, which "makes the winds to blow and the waters to flow only by shining."
- **A system is alluring because it promises calculable results.** A "system of education" is "pledged to more definite calculable results"; it works by "the observing of rules until the habit of doing certain things... is confirmed." Things genuinely learnable on a system include shorthand, dancing, accountancy — "how to pass examinations."
- **System is tempting because watchfulness is hard.** A definite scheme "is more agreeable than the constant watchfulness, the unforeseen action, called for when the whole of a child's existence is to be used as the means of his education." The preference for system arises "from the sluggishness of human nature."
- **The child is not a machine.** "If a human being were a machine, education could do no more for him than to set him in action in prescribed ways." But the educator "has to deal with a self-acting, self-developing being" — so a system, which produces mechanical action, is the wrong instrument for the central work.
- **System has a real but subordinate place.** "System is highly useful as an instrument of education" — for the mechanical, drillable parts. The error is the **'system _of_ education'**: letting a system that develops the child "in one direction — of the muscles, of the memory, of the reasoning faculty" stand in for "a complete all-round education."
- **A good method can decay into a system.** Mason's example is the Kindergarten: a genuine method "conceived and perfected by large-hearted educators," but "what a miserable wooden _system_ does it become in the hands of ignorant practitioners!" The decay happens whenever the practices outlive the principles that gave them life. (See [[entities/friedrich-froebel|Froebel]].)
- **A few principles cover the whole field.** Method is not endless improvisation: "a few broad essential principles cover the whole field, and these once fully laid hold of, it is as easy and natural to act upon them" as on any other known fact.

## In Practice

- The educator forms "a mental image of the end" first, then lets a small set of principles govern every decision rather than adopting a ready-made scheme.
- Drillable skills (handwriting, tables, scales) may be carried on systematically; the central work — the feeding of the mind, the forming of the person — is conducted as method.
- A practice is judged by whether the principle behind it is still alive in the practitioner: the same Kindergarten "gift" is method in one teacher's hands and dead system in another's.
- [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|Masterly inactivity]] is method's restraint — the refusal to over-manage — against the over-organising tendency of a system.

## Variants & Related Concepts

- [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|Masterly Inactivity]] — method's characteristic restraint.
- [[concepts/habit-formation|Habit Formation]] — habit is the legitimate "system" within a method: useful for the mechanical, dangerous when mistaken for the whole.
