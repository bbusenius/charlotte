# The Habit of Attention

## Definition

In [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]]'s system, **attention** is "the act by which the whole mental force is applied to the subject in hand" — not a faculty of the mind but a single act, and one that can be trained into a **habit**. The habit of attention is, for Mason, the most important of all the [[concepts/habit-formation|habits]] a parent forms, "because the highest intellectual gifts depend for their value upon the measure in which their owner has cultivated the habit of attention." _Home Education_, Part IV, is the wiki's source for the doctrine.

## Core Ideas

- **The mind drifts by the law of association.** Ideas course through the mind ceaselessly; left to themselves they follow association, not purpose — Mason's example is the child who, asked about glass, wanders to Cinderella's slipper, to his godmother, to a boat, to Uncle Harry. The law of association "is a good servant and a bad master."
- **Wandering attention is the real waste of the schoolroom.** "The children are thinking all the time about something else than their lessons." The harm is not only lost time but the forming of "a desultory habit of mind" — a reduced capacity for mental effort.
- **Attention is cultivated, beginning in infancy.** A baby has observation but no attention; the mother holds the infant's eyes on a plaything for "fully a couple of minutes" — "his first lesson in attention." Later she invests each object with interest (the daisy as "day's-eye") so the child looks "long enough at one thing to get a real acquaintance with it."
- **The supreme practical principle: short, varied, timed lessons.** Lessons are kept short — "seldom more than twenty minutes in length for children under eight" — because the sense that time is limited keeps the wits alert. Lessons are alternated so that one power rests while another works: "a 'thinking' lesson first, and a 'painstaking' lesson to follow." A written time-table makes the work definite.
- **The right motive is the desire for knowledge.** Mason allows emulation, the desire of approbation, and a "natural reward" (work done quickly earns leisure), but warns against letting any of these displace the child's native appetite for knowledge, "which is equally natural, and is adequate for all the purposes of education."
- **Self-compelled attention is the goal.** As the child grows he is taught to bring his own will to bear — "to feel a certain triumph in compelling himself to fix his thoughts." Here the habit of attention hands over to the [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]].
- **Inattention, not work, causes "over-pressure."** Mason holds that children are tired less by what they do than by what they fail to do — a lesson half-attended becomes "a bugbear, continually wanted henceforth and never there." The cure for the child who "moons" is wholesome firmness, not sympathy.

## In Practice

- A written time-table; each lesson a definite task to be finished in a set time.
- Lessons short (ten to twenty minutes for the young) and deliberately alternated in kind.
- The teacher makes each subject genuinely interesting rather than coaxing attention with marks; marks, where used, are given for conduct, not cleverness, so no child is left with "a rankling sense of injustice."
- Never let a child dawdle or "moon" over a book; when he grows stupid over a lesson, change the lesson.
- Rewards and punishments are kept to the **natural or relative consequences of conduct** — prompt work earns leisure as a right; they are not arbitrary bribes.

## Variants & Related Concepts

- [[concepts/habit-formation|Habit Formation]] — the habit of attention is the most important single application of the general doctrine.
- [[concepts/single-reading|Single Reading]] — the operational expression of the habit: the child attends fully because he knows there will be no second reading.
- [[concepts/narration|Narration]] — what the attentive child does next; narration both uses and verifies the attention.
- [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]] — "power of will implies power of attention"; the trained habit becomes self-compelled attention.

> [!note] _Home Education_ (1906) treats attention as a **habit to be cultivated**, while the later [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|_Philosophy of Education_]] (1923) calls attention "a natural function which requires no effort." These are complementary, not contradictory: the natural power of attention exists in every child, but without the habit it drifts by association. Mason says as much here — "attention is, to a great extent, the product of the educated mind."

## Key Proponents / Origins

- [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] — the doctrine and the practical rules are hers; she quotes an unnamed authority that attention is "within the reach of every one, and should be made the primary object of all mental discipline."
