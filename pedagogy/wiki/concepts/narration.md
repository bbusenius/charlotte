# Narration

## Definition

The method, in [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]]'s system, by which a child knows what he has read: the child tells back, orally or (from age ~10) in writing, what was just read — without prompts, without re-reading, without interruption. Narration replaces comprehension questions, summaries, note-taking, and in most cases formal memorisation. Mason treats it as the single universal method across every subject and every age.

## Core Ideas

- **"Whatever a child or grown-up person can tell, that we may be sure he knows, and what he cannot tell, he does not know."** Narration is both method and assessment.
- **No interruption, no correction during the telling.** Other children may correct afterwards. The teacher's role is to read well and look sympathetically.
- **Mind memory, not word memory.** Mason invokes Bergson's distinction: narration after a [[concepts/single-reading|single attentive reading]] is not rote — it is the mind reconstructing the passage from its own grasp, and so the reconstruction is durable, often verbatim without having been "memorised."
- **Composition is a by-product.** Mason holds that what is taught in other schools as "composition" develops of its own accord from years of narration. In Forms V–VI, she writes, teachers need only offer "a point or two" of University-tutor-style advice; the style is already formed.
- **Narration in a foreign language works the same way.** At the [[entities/house-of-education|House of Education]], students narrate Daudet — heard once, by ear — in French, immediately, in turn. Mason treats this as demonstrative proof of the method.

## In Practice

- Form I (ages 6–9): paragraph-by-paragraph oral narration; the teacher reads, the child tells.
- Form II (9–12): chapter-by-chapter narration, increasingly of books the child has read himself; written narration begins.
- Forms III–VI: full written narrations (short essays, verse, dialogues, letters-in-the-manner-of) after a term's reading; terminal examinations are simply written narrations under formal conditions.
- Shakespeare, Scott, Plutarch, Milton, Pope, the Gospels — all narrated by children from age 9 upward.
- The teacher is **forbidden** from explaining the text pre-emptively, asking leading questions, or paraphrasing for the child.

## Variants & Related Concepts

- [[concepts/single-reading|Single Reading]] — the rule that pairs with narration: no second chance to attend, and no need for one.
- [[concepts/living-books|Living Books]] — narration only works on prose worth telling back; twaddle cannot be narrated because there is nothing to narrate.
- [[concepts/children-are-born-persons|Children are Born Persons]] — narration is a grown-up activity the child is already capable of, not a training exercise.
- [[concepts/20-principles-of-education|20 Principles]] — Principles 14–15 formalise the rule.

**Modern analogue:** narration is a form of _retrieval practice_ in the cognitive-science sense — recall from memory strengthens the memory — though Mason has no notion of spacing or interleaving. Whether narration benefits from spaced repetition is an open empirical question.

## Key Proponents / Origins

- [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] — the universal application is hers, and an early one: the method is set out at chapter length as early as [[sources/home-education|_Home Education_]] (1906, from 1885 lectures), where narration is "an art... _there_, in every child's mind, waiting to be discovered." Mason notes that narration ("telling") was a normal Elizabethan social skill (she cites _Henry VIII_) and suspects it was more practised generally in the 16th–17th centuries than in her day.

## Mentioned In

- [[sources/home-education|Home Education]] — Volume I (1906), the wiki's earliest source for the method. Part V, Chapter IX ("The Art of Narrating"): "'Let him narrate'; and the child narrates." Mason gives the lesson method — read consecutively from a well-chosen book; a little talk about the last lesson, but no explanation and no forestalling of the narrative; read an episode of two or three pages; then call on the children to narrate, in turn — and the rule that under-sixes narrate only "when and what [they have] a mind to." Bible lessons and reading for older children apply the same method.
