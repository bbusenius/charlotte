# Habit Formation

## Definition

[[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]]'s central practical doctrine: **"the formation of habits _is_ education, and Education is the formation of habits."** A habit, in Mason's account, is a settled way of acting, thinking, or feeling that has become so easy it carries the child along without effort or decision. The deliberate laying-down of good habits is the chief instrument by which a parent or teacher works — "the discipline of habit" is one of the three legitimate instruments named in [[concepts/education-is-atmosphere-discipline-life|Education is an Atmosphere, a Discipline, a Life]]. _Home Education_, Part III ("Habit is Ten Natures"), is the wiki's primary source for the doctrine.

## Core Ideas

- **"Habit is ten natures."** Nature — the sum of a child's inherited and constitutional tendencies — is "incalculably strong." But habit "is not only as strong, but _tenfold_ as strong," and so habit is "a stronger than he, able to overcome this strong man armed." Habit is the lever by which a parent "may make almost anything he chooses of his child."
- **Habit runs on the lines of nature — or against them.** Left alone, habit simply confirms nature: the cowardly child _habitually_ lies, the generous child has a _habit_ of giving. To be an instrument of education, habit must be made to "work contrary to nature, or at any rate, independently of her."
- **Habit has a physical seat.** Mason takes this from [[entities/william-carpenter|Dr Carpenter]]'s _Mental Physiology_: growing tissue forms itself to the modes of action required of it, and the brain is no exception. Every familiar train of thought wears a "rut" in the nervous substance into which thought afterwards "runs lightly of its own accord." The enormous strength of habit is, on this theory, the physical record left in the brain.
- **Habit governs almost everything.** "It is habit... which will govern ninety-nine one-hundredths of the child's life." We act and think habitually because "the effort of decision is the most exhausting effort of life"; without habit, life "would not be worth having." The question is never _whether_ the child is a creature of habit but _which_ habits he is formed in.
- **The parent lays down "lines of habit."** Habit is to a life as rails to a locomotive: it is easier to run on the laid line than off it. The parent's serious, responsible task is to choose the tracks and lay the rails "invitingly smooth and easy."
- **A habit is a delight in itself.** Because it is pleasant to repeat an action without effort, habit-training rewards itself — "like the laying out of a penny with the certainty of the immediate return of a pound."
- **The most powerful habits are caught, not taught.** Cleanliness, order, neatness, regularity, courtesy — these the child "imbibes" from the [[concepts/education-is-atmosphere-discipline-life|atmosphere]] of the home, passively, "as the air he breathes." "Nine times out of ten we begin to do a thing because we see some one else do it."

## In Practice

Mason's worked example is the habit of shutting the door ("Shut the Door After You"):

- **Begin with the child's goodwill.** A few earnest words enlist the child's own (still feeble) will on the side of the habit — "I want you to remember something with all your might."
- **Tact, watchfulness, persistence.** The mother never lets a lapse pass and never lets the matter become "a cause of friction"; she is the child's "friendly ally" against his own forgetful memory, not his accuser.
- **One habit at a time.** The mother "devotes herself to the formation of one habit at a time, doing no more than keep watch over those already formed." A child who starts life with "twenty good habits" begins with capital.
- **Beware the dangerous stage.** Once a habit is nearly formed, the mother's misplaced pity is fatal: "Poor child, I'll let him off this once." The "little relaxation" is not a pause but the beginning of a contrary habit, which must then be overcome all over again. "To form a good habit is the work of a few weeks; to guard it is a work of incessant... care."
- **One custom overcometh another.** A bad habit (dawdling, ill temper) is cured only by patiently substituting the contrary habit — Mason compares it to nursing a child through an illness.

## Variants & Related Concepts

- [[concepts/education-is-atmosphere-discipline-life|Education is an Atmosphere, a Discipline, a Life]] — habit-formation is the substance of the "discipline" term.
- [[concepts/habit-of-attention|The Habit of Attention]] — the single most important habit, and the bridge to all intellectual work.
- [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]] — habit is both the ally and (when bad) the frustrater of the will; good habits free the will from routine choices.
- [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|Masterly Inactivity]] — once good habits are laid, the parent can safely "let children alone"; habit, not supervision, keeps them on track.
- [[concepts/children-are-born-persons|Children are Born Persons]] — habit-training works _with_ a person, enlisting his will, rather than conditioning an animal.

**Modern note:** Mason's behavioural claims — habits are durable, largely automatic, physically instantiated, and best changed by substitution — are broadly consistent with later habit research. Her specific physiology (new brain tissue "growing to" a habit, drawn from [[entities/william-carpenter|Carpenter]]) is a Victorian theory and should be read as period mechanism, not settled neuroscience.

## Key Proponents / Origins

- [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] — the pedagogical doctrine is hers; she names the maxim "Habit is ten natures" as one she heard repeatedly "delivered from the pulpit."
- [[entities/william-carpenter|William Carpenter]] — his _Mental Physiology_ "was perhaps the first which gave me the clue I was in search of"; the physiological half of the doctrine is Carpenter's. Mason also quotes Huxley on "artificial reflex actions" acquired by drill.
