# Charlotte Mason's Copywork Technique

Charlotte Mason's word for copywork in [[sources/home-education|_Home Education_]] is **transcription**. She places it in the early writing sequence for children of seven or eight: not letter-writing, not dictation, but "transcription, slow and beautiful work." The exercise is meant to train handwriting, spelling, attention, and taste at once.

The exact technique is **visualized transcription**:

1. The child is given a short, worthy passage, ideally a favourite verse chosen from a poem.
2. The child looks carefully at a word.
3. The child closes the eyes and sees a mental picture of the word.
4. The child writes the word from memory, not by glancing back letter by letter.
5. The work is done slowly and beautifully, at first on double-ruled lines in small text-hand.
6. The lesson stops after ten to fifteen minutes, before fatigue produces careless work.

Mason states the method compactly: "Children should be encouraged to look at the word, see a picture of it with their eyes shut, and then write from memory." This is why transcription is also her introduction to spelling. The child is trained to hold the correct visual form of a word in the mind before writing it.

## Once, If Perfect

Mason's aim is not repeated copying for volume. In the preceding writing chapter she says the lesson is "not so many lines" and not "a page of writing," but **a single line** copied as exactly as possible from the model. The child may have to write several lines before succeeding, but the target is one perfect execution, not a page filled with repetitions.

Practically, this means:

1. If the line is done well, stop.
2. If it is imperfect, have the child notice the fault and try again.
3. Repetition is remedial: it serves perfect execution, not rote drill.

This fits Mason's broader rule that no work should be assigned which the child cannot execute perfectly, and then perfection should be required as a matter of course.

## Why It Matters

Mason does not treat copywork as mechanical copying. Its purpose is not to fill a page, imitate a printed headline, or correct a long list of mistakes afterward. The point is to prevent wrong forms from being seen and fixed in memory. This is the same principle behind her prepared dictation method: spelling depends on the eye's habit of taking a clear image of the correctly printed word.

So, in Mason terms, good copywork is short, beautiful, chosen from living language, and executed from a mental image. It is handwriting practice, but also spelling practice and attention training.
