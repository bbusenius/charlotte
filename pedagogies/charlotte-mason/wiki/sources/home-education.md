# Home Education

**Author:** [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte M. Mason]]
**Year:** 1906 — the **fifth edition, "Revised and Enlarged."** The greater part of the book was first delivered as "Lectures to Ladies" in 1885 and published in 1886; the preface to the fourth edition is signed "Scale How, Ambleside, 1905." The ingested text is Project Gutenberg eBook #71087, set from the 1906 Kegan Paul, Trench, Trübner & Co. edition.
**Type:** book — **Volume I** of Mason's six-volume _Home Education Series_. The series is named after this volume, "and not as dealing, wholly or principally, with 'Home' as opposed to 'School' education." The wiki's other Mason source — [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|_Towards a Philosophy of Education_]] (1923) — is **Volume VI** of the same series. The wiki therefore now holds the first and the last volumes, written ~37 years apart.

## Overview

Mason's first major book, and the foundation of everything that follows. Where the 1923 _Philosophy of Education_ is a late, compact synthesis, _Home Education_ is the early, expansive, intensely practical original — addressed to mothers, concerned specifically with **the education of children under nine**, and written to suggest "a method of education resting upon a basis of natural law." Its conceptual spine is already the spine of the whole system — [[concepts/children-are-born-persons|children are born persons]], [[concepts/narration|narration]] after a [[concepts/single-reading|single reading]] of [[concepts/living-books|living books]], [[concepts/science-of-relations|education as the science of relations]], the [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]] — which means that spine was laid down in the 1885 lectures, not invented in 1923. But _Home Education_ also carries large bodies of material absent from the wiki's later-Mason pages: a physiological grounding for education (brain, blood, food, air, rest); a sustained doctrine of [[concepts/habit-formation|habit formation]] ("Habit is ten natures"); a programme of [[concepts/nature-study|out-of-door life and nature study]]; the doctrine of [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|masterly inactivity]]; and chapter-by-chapter **methods for teaching every early-years subject** — reading, writing, spelling, arithmetic, history, geography, French, art. It is the most operationally detailed Mason source in the wiki.

## Structure

A double preface, six numbered Parts, and four appendices.

- **Preface to the 'Home Education' Series** — states the want of "a philosophy of education" and appends an **eighteen-point numbered synopsis** of Mason's theory. This is the direct precursor of the [[concepts/20-principles-of-education|20 Principles]] of 1923; the list in this 1906 edition has **eighteen** points, not twenty (see Open Questions).
- **Preface to the Fourth Edition** — states the aim ("a method of education resting upon a basis of natural law"), the age range (infancy to nine), and the debt to Dr Carpenter's _Mental Physiology_ for the chapters on habit.
- **Part I — Some Preliminary Considerations**: children as "public trusts"; [[concepts/method-vs-system|a method, not a system]]; the child's estate ("a prince committed to the fostering care of peasants"); the Gospel "code of education" — Offend not, Despise not, Hinder not; **Conditions of Healthy Brain-Activity** (the physiology of brain, blood, food, air, rest); "the Reign of Law" in education.
- **Part II — Out-of-Door Life for the Children**: four-to-six hours daily in the open; "sight-seeing" and "picture-painting"; flowers, trees, living creatures; the nature note-book and calendar; out-of-door geography; "the child and Mother-Nature"; games; bad-weather walks; scouting and "bird-stalking"; country air. See [[concepts/nature-study|nature study]].
- **Part III — "Habit is Ten Natures"**: education as the formation of [[concepts/habit-formation|habits]]; children have no self-compelling power; what "Nature" is, and how habit may supplant it; the physiology of habit; the forming of a habit ("Shut the door after you"); infant habits — cleanliness, order, regularity; physical exercises.
- **Part IV — Some Habits of Mind, Some Moral Habits**: the [[concepts/habit-of-attention|habit of attention]] (and application, thinking, imagining, remembering, perfect execution); obedience; truthfulness; sweet temper.
- **Part V — Lessons as Instruments of Education**: the matter and method of lessons; a critique of the Kindergarten and of [[entities/friedrich-froebel|Froebel]]'s system; then chapter methods for reading, recitation, [[concepts/narration|narration]], writing, transcription, spelling and dictation, composition, Bible, arithmetic, natural philosophy, geography, history, grammar, French (by M. Gouin's "Series" method), and pictorial art.
- **Part VI — The Will, the Conscience, the Divine Life in the Child**: the "government of Mansoul"; the will as executive power; wilfulness as the _want_ of will; conscience as lawgiver and judge; the divine life and the parent's task of presenting "the idea of God."
- **Appendices** — A: a book list; B: study questions for PNEU students; C & D: the actual termly programmes and examination papers for a child of seven (Class IB) and of nine (Class II), with specimen answers.

## Key Claims

- **Education rests on natural law.** Education is not a contrivance but the following of law — physical, intellectual, moral, all of which Mason regards as divine law. To know the law is to be able to act on a few broad principles rather than improvising endlessly.
- **A method, not a system.** A [[concepts/method-vs-system|method]] is a way to an end, natural and all-pervading, pressing every circumstance into service; a _system_ produces calculable but mechanical results and "is mischievous, as producing only mechanical action instead of the vital growth and movement of a living being."
- **The child is already a person.** Principle 1 of the synopsis: "Children are born _persons_." The book's three Gospel laws — Offend not, Despise not, Hinder not — are all charges against the adult who fails to respect the personality already present.
- **The physical is the lowest but necessary round of the ladder.** Mason devotes a long early chapter to the physiology of the brain: lessons must come after rest, not after heavy meals; the brain needs varied work, abundant well-oxygenated blood, fresh air, sunshine. "Intellectual, moral, even spiritual life and progress depend greatly upon physical conditions."
- **Out-of-door life is the first education.** The young child's "business in the world" is to find out all he can through his five senses; he should spend four to six hours a day outside, much of it let alone, forming a first-hand acquaintance with nature. See [[concepts/nature-study|nature study]].
- **Education is the formation of habits.** "Habit is ten natures." Habit runs on the lines of nature but can be made a lever to supplant it; the parent lays down "lines of habit" as rails for the child's life to run on. Mason grounds this in the physiology of [[entities/william-carpenter|Dr Carpenter]] — repeated thought wears a "rut" in the brain tissue. See [[concepts/habit-formation|habit formation]].
- **The habit of attention is supreme.** "Within the reach of every one, and... the primary object of all mental discipline." Lessons are kept short (ten to twenty minutes for the young) and varied so that one power rests while another works; the [[concepts/habit-of-attention|habit of attention]] is the linchpin of all later learning.
- **Lessons must furnish living ideas.** "A morning in which a child receives no new idea is a morning wasted." An idea is a living, growing thing ("a spiritual germ"); a single fruitful idea is worth "bushels of information." [[concepts/living-books|Living books]], not "diluted" twaddle, are the food.
- **Children narrate by nature.** [[concepts/narration|Narration]] "is _there_, in every child's mind, waiting to be discovered" — the natural way a child knows what he has read. One reading is enough; the child tells it back without correction or leading questions.
- **Reading is taught by whole, interesting words.** Mason teaches reading from nursery rhymes cut into loose-word boxes ("Cock Robin," "Little Pussy"): a child learns words by sight because they carry an interesting idea, and learns the powers of letters by word-building on alternate days. The arbitrary symbols a child must learn are "words, and not letters."
- **Spelling is a habit of visualising words; composition comes by nature.** Bad spelling is a failure of the eye; the cure is to picture the word and to take dictation by a method that never lets a wrong form be seen. Formal "composition" exercises for the young are "an educational futility" — composition develops of itself out of years of narration and wide reading.
- **The will is exercised by the diversion of attention.** Strong "wilfulness" is the _want_ of will; true will is the power to change one's own thoughts. "Are you cross? Change your thoughts." This [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]] should be taught to children as the secret of self-management.
- **The parent's highest task is to present the idea of God.** Like a bee carrying pollen, the parent can only "deposit... within reach of the soul of the child some fruitful idea of God"; the living Word does the rest. Christianity is "personal loyalty... to our adorable Chief."
- **Wise letting-alone.** Repeatedly: the gardener prunes his peach tree "a small fraction of the tree's life"; the rest is sun and rain. Much of education is [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|"masterly inactivity"]] — not the perpetual "cackle" of teaching, but giving the child time and space "to wonder—and grow."

## Notable Quotes

> "Children are born _persons_."
> — Principle 1 of the eighteen-point synopsis; the keystone, present here in 1906 exactly as in 1923.

> "_Take heed that ye_ OFFEND _not_—DESPISE _not_—HINDER _not_—_one of these little ones_. So run the three educational laws of the New Testament."
> — Mason's "code of education in the Gospels," Part I; the negative frame of the whole book.

> "The formation of habits _is_ education, and _Education is the formation of habits_."
> — Part III; the thesis of [[concepts/habit-formation|habit formation]] and the most-quoted line of the book.

> "Method... presses the most unlikely matters into service to bring about [the] end; but with no more tiresome mechanism than the sun employs when it makes the winds to blow and the waters to flow only by shining."
> — Part I, on [[concepts/method-vs-system|method versus system]].

> "Never be within doors when you can _rightly_ be without."
> — Part II; the rule of [[concepts/nature-study|out-of-door life]].

> "Of the evils of modern education few are worse than this—that the perpetual cackle of his elders leaves the poor child not a moment of time, nor an inch of space, wherein to wonder—and grow."
> — Part II; the case for [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|masterly inactivity]].

> "'Let him narrate'; and the child narrates, fluently, copiously, in ordered sequence, with fit and graphic details... so soon as he can speak with ease. This amazing gift... is allowed to lie fallow in their education."
> — Part V, "The Art of Narrating."

> "It is a marvellous and beautiful thing to perceive an idea... developing within you of its own accord... fairly implant an _idea_ in the child's mind, and it will secrete its own food, grow, and bear fruit."
> — Part V; the doctrine of ideas as living things, the root of [[concepts/knowledge-as-food|knowledge as food]].

> "Are you cross? Change your thoughts. Are you tired of trying? Change your thoughts... there is a power within you, your own will, which will enable you to turn your attention from thoughts that make you unhappy _and wrong_, to thoughts that make you happy _and right_."
> — Part VI; the [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]] put into words a child can use.

> "The parent is little better in this matter than the witless bee; it is his part to deposit, so to speak, within reach of the soul of the child some fruitful idea of God; the immature soul makes no effort towards that idea, but the living Word reaches down, touches the soul,—and there is _life_."
> — Part VI, "The Divine Life in the Child."

## Connections to Existing Wiki

- **Same author, same series, opposite ends.** _Home Education_ is Volume I; [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|_Towards a Philosophy of Education_]] (Books I and II) is Volume VI. The wiki previously held only the late synthesis and described Mason as "single-author-two-volume." She is now better described as a single author across a long career: the 1885 lectures and the 1923 valedictory bracket nearly four decades, and the conceptual spine is demonstrably the same at both ends.
- **The spine predates the synthesis.** Narration, single reading, living books, science of relations, the Way of the Will, the triad of atmosphere/discipline/life, and the formation of habits are all present in 1906. The 1923 book refined and systematised; it did not originate. Concept pages previously sourced only to the 1923 volume have been retrofitted with _Home Education_ as the earlier source.
- **It fills the early-years and physiological gaps.** The 1923-sourced pages carry nothing on brain physiology, on out-of-door life, or on the under-nine years. _Home Education_ supplies all three, plus the [[concepts/habit-formation|habit-formation]] machinery that the triad's "discipline" term only gestures at.
- **It is the operational manual.** For the homeschool-hub curriculum skills, this is the most directly usable Mason source: it gives concrete lesson methods (the loose-word reading boxes, prepared dictation, arithmetic with beans, picture-talk, history from a single chronicle) rather than principles alone.

## Open Questions & Gaps

> [!question] The synopsis in this 1906 edition has **eighteen** numbered principles; the 1923 _Philosophy of Education_ has **twenty**. What changed between them, and when? The 1906 list is more polemically anti-Herbartian (it names the "apperception mass" and the "doctrine of interest" directly) and ends on the unity of intellectual and spiritual life; the 1923 list ends on "we live by admiration, hope and love." Tracing the evolution would require Volumes II–V of the series, none of which are yet ingested.

> [!question] Mason's doctrine of [[concepts/habit-formation|habit]] rests explicitly on [[entities/william-carpenter|Dr Carpenter]]'s _Mental Physiology_ — Victorian associationist psychology, in which repeated thought literally "wears a rut" in growing brain tissue. How much of this survives modern neuroscience? The behavioural claim (habits are durable and physically instantiated) is broadly vindicated; the specific mechanism (new tissue "growing to" a habit) is a period theory. The wiki should not treat the physiology as settled.

> [!question] _Home Education_'s programme assumes a mother free to spend four-to-six hours daily out of doors with her children, with nurses, country access, and leisure. Mason half-acknowledges this ("I venture to suggest, not what is practicable in any household, but what seems to me _absolutely best_") — yet the later [[concepts/liberal-education-for-all|liberal-education-for-all]] thesis insists the method is class-independent. Is the out-of-door programme of Volume I compatible with that later universalism, or is it the most class-bound part of the system?

> [!question] Mason teaches reading by a hybrid of whole-word recognition (the "Cock Robin" / "Little Pussy" loose-word boxes) and phonic word-building on alternate days, explicitly rejecting a pure synthetic-phonics drill as suited to German but not to anomalous English. Where does this sit in the modern phonics-versus-whole-language debate?

> [!question] The phrase [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|"masterly inactivity"]] appears here but is given a full chapter only in Volume III, _School Education_. The wiki holds Volumes I and VI; the four middle volumes (_Parents and Children_, _School Education_, _Ourselves_, _Some Studies in the Formation of Character_) remain un-ingested and would close real gaps — masterly inactivity, the will and conscience material, and the formation of character among them.
