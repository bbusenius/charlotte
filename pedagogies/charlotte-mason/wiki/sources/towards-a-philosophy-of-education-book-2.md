# An Essay Towards a Philosophy of Education — Book II

**Author:** [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte M. Mason]]
**Year:** 1923 (volume 6 of the _Home Education Series_, published the year of her death)
**Type:** book — the second, applied half of the 1923 volume
**Source scope:** This page covers **Book II — "Theory Applied,"** the applied half of the complete source file. Book I ("Some Preliminary Considerations") has its own sectional summary: [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|Book I]].

## Overview

If Book I lays out the theory (20 Principles, a doctrine of the person, a doctrine of knowledge), Book II argues the political and practical thesis those principles entail: **a liberal, book-centred, literary education is the right of every child, and the same curriculum should run in the village Council school, the Public School, and the continuation class for young wage-earners from fourteen to eighteen.** Mason works through each institutional setting — Elementary Schools (Ch. I), Secondary Schools (Ch. II), Continuation Schools (Ch. III) — and then steps back in a long four-chapter essay, "The Basis of National Strength" (Ch. IV), to argue that [[concepts/knowledge-as-food|knowledge is the proper food of mind]], that [[concepts/act-of-knowing|the act of knowing]] is self-performed by the child on literary material presented once, and that the failure to feed mind is the hidden cause of political and industrial unrest. A closing "Supplementary" — "[[concepts/too-wide-a-mesh|Too Wide a Mesh]]" — dissects the tragic case of two brilliant Public School "Twins" who left school "wholly uneducated," as a last illustration that even elite schooling serves only the top dozen pupils and fails the rest.

Book II is therefore the _social_ and _polemical_ volume. Its claims are empirical (Drighlington, Gloucestershire Council schools, the PUS programmes run across class lines), its tone confident, and its target the Edwardian–post-war English education establishment.

## Structure

- **Ch. I — A Liberal Education in Elementary Schools.** The Drighlington experiment; the PUS method carried out in Council schools. Establishes the empirical basis for the book's social thesis.
- **Ch. II — A Liberal Education in Secondary Schools.** Critique of Public School classics-and-games monoculture; the case for "inclusive not exclusive" education. Contains the iconic passage on the "common basis of thought" — Sancho Panza, Elsinore, Excalibur, Rosinante as the property of every English child.
- **Ch. III — The Scope of Continuation Schools.** A comparative essay: Mason contrasts the utilitarian Munich continuation schools of Dr. Kerschensteiner with the Danish People's High Schools of [[entities/nfs-grundtvig|N. F. S. Grundtvig]] and argues that England should follow Denmark, not Germany. The seven or eight weekly hours of Continuation School time must be given to the humanities, not to technical training ("which is--work").
- **Ch. IV — The Basis of National Strength: A Liberal Education from a National Standpoint** (six numbered sections, originally published in _The Times_, 1912):
  - I. Knowledge — "knowledge is not a store, but rather a state that a person remains within or drops out of"
  - II. Letters, Knowledge and Virtue — answering a correspondent on the value of classics vs. English literature
  - III. Knowledge, Reason, and Rebellion — [[concepts/way-of-the-will|reason is a servant of the will]]; industrial unrest is the fruit of reason without knowledge
  - IV. New and Old Conceptions of Knowledge — the mediaeval "Florentine" view (Simone Memmi's fresco in Santa Maria Novella) of all knowledge as proceeding from Divine teaching
  - V. Education and the Fullness of Life — critique of "I must live my life" (Ibsen's _Doll's House_, the Bonnot brigand); mind is the undernourished faculty
  - VI. Knowledge in Literary Form — the whole of a child's instruction must come through literature
- **Supplementary — "Too Wide a Mesh."** The fable of the Public-School "Twins" who left school uneducated.

## Key Claims

- **The PUS method works in Council (state) elementary schools.** Mason repeatedly cites Drighlington (Yorkshire) and a mining-village school of 40 children as demonstrations. Council-school pupils in Standard VII read three or four volumes of history, two or three of citizenship and ethics, literature contemporary with the history read, three or four on natural history and science, plus the Bible — and answer examination questions covering the whole term with no revision.
- **A single reading plus [[concepts/narration|narration]] suffices.** A second reading is "fatal because no one can give full attention to that which he has heard before." Mason repeatedly invokes the experiment: put yourself to sleep by mentally telling back a chapter of Jane Austen after reading it once, and you will find you have it whole.
- **[[concepts/knowledge-as-food|Knowledge is the food of the mind]].** This metaphor is the load-bearing argument of Book II — "flour is the material of bread; knowledge is the material of education." Substitutes (marks, prizes, scholarships, "aniseed drops") stay the stomach without feeding the child. The mind, like the body, needs a regular, generous ration.
- **There is an [[concepts/act-of-knowing|act of knowing]] that only the child can perform.** Information conveyed by a teacher is "a veneer laid on the surface of a child's nature" until the child has performed this act — an inner question the mind puts to itself ("What next?") and answered through narration. This is the deepest single idea of the book.
- **The Public School regime fails the average boy.** Ch. II and the "Twins" supplement converge on this: classics-and-games turns out a "dozen head boys" but leaves the many "wholly uneducated." The remedy is not to abolish classics but to "broaden the base and narrow the bounds": keep classics for the top Form, but give wide English-language reading to the lower Forms, where boys (and girls) are capable of far more than is offered them.
- **A "common basis of thought" is a political good.** Democracy cannot function when the classes share no books. If the masses know Sancho Panza, Excalibur, Mrs. Jellaby, bogbean, redstart, the classes must know them too, "with easy intimacy" — and vice versa. Literary allusion is a bond, ignorance a barrier.
- **Continuation schools should teach the humanities, not trades.** Mason contrasts [[entities/nfs-grundtvig|Grundtvig]]'s Danish People's High Schools (residential, literary, Christian, and credited with "making Danes the first farmers in Europe") with the Munich Continuation Schools (utilitarian, technical, narrow). English continuation schools must not "betray" their pupils by giving them more of their employer's training; the 7–8 hours must be spent on mind-food.
- **[[concepts/way-of-the-will|Reason serves the will]], not truth.** Expanding Book I's critique of Marx: bodies of working men now "act with a momentum" that depends on whether they have knowledge. Reason without knowledge, following a false premise, produces Rebellion — not because the men are vicious but because reason is infallibly logical in defense of _any_ premise the will accepts.
- **The faculty psychology is wrong.** Repeatedly and polemically: there are no "faculties" to develop. There is one mind, "alert, self-active, discriminating, logical," which functions correctly when fed. Gymnastics of the mind ("intellectual Swedish Drill") is no substitute for food.
- **All knowledge must reach the mind "in literary form."** Textbooks, abstracts, digests, compilations — these deal with "that curious outsider, the verbal memory" and do not touch mind. Even science: French scientific prose, Mason says, has "the exquisite prose" that makes science educative; English science texts are "barbed wire entanglements." This extends to geography, history, civics — every subject must come in literary clothing.
- **The [[concepts/too-wide-a-mesh|big-mesh exam system]] catches only the top fish.** The "Twins" essay is the sharp edge of the critique: even an affectionate, privileged schooling that its pupils remember with gratitude can leave them "invincibly ignorant," labouring in adulthood to remedy what school should have given them.
- **The examination system Mason proposes is counter-intuitive.** No revision. Too much ground covered to allow "looking up." Single reading, narration, terminal exam over 15+ subjects, no cramming. The examination is a permanent record, not a sorting device.
- **The Bible belongs in the curriculum — as literature read by the method.** The teacher reads a passage (with any geographical or doctrinal context given _before_ the reading), the children narrate. No moralising, no leading questions, no drawing out of lessons — the method is applied to Scripture exactly as to any other literature. Mason insists this is _more_, not less, religiously serious than the devotional alternative.
- **Drawing is expression, not self-expression.** Children draw "not themselves, but what they can see and what they conceive" — scenes visualised in the term's reading, natural figures and objects. This foreshadows her opposition to the Rousseauian pedagogy that elevates self-expression.

## Notable Quotes

> "Knowledge is the material of education, as flour is the material of bread."
> — Ch. IV.I; the thesis of the whole second half of the book.

> "Knowledge is not a store, but rather a state that a person remains within or drops out of."
> — Ch. IV.II; Mason's most compact statement of why examination-focussed schooling fails.

> "Let the child tell what he has read in whole or in part on the instant, and again, in an examination paper months later."
> — Ch. II; the operational rule.

> "Information does not become knowledge unless a child perform the 'act of knowing' without the intervention of another personality."
> — Ch. II; the irreducible core of Mason's anti-didactic claim.

> "The mind refuses to know anything except what reaches it in more or less literary form."
> — Ch. II.

> "We may take knowledge roughly as of three sorts — knowledge of God, to be got first-hand through the sacred writings; knowledge of man, to be arrived at through history, poetry, tale; … and every youth should know something of the flowers of the field, the birds of the air, the stars in their courses."
> — Ch. III; the [[concepts/knowledge-of-god-man-universe|threefold curriculum]] restated for Continuation Schools.

> "A misguiding theory neatly put is more dangerous than an ill-example."
> — Ch. IV.V; apropos Ibsen and the cult of self-expression.

> "Reason is a man's servant, not his master, and behaves like a good and faithful servant — a sort of 'Caleb Balderstone,' ready to lie royally in his master's behoof — and bring logical demonstration of any premiss which the will chooses to entertain."
> — Ch. IV.III; the political expansion of Book I's Chapter IX.

> "The net is cast wide no doubt and brings in a mighty haul but the meshes are so wide that it will only retain big fishes."
> — "Too Wide a Mesh"; the image that gives the supplementary essay its title.

> "All knowledge for all men." — Comenius, quoted in Ch. III as the hope Mason believes the PUS has shown to be practicable.

> "The future of England depends largely upon Secondary schools; let the Heads of these lay out a liberal field of study and astonishingly fair things will grow in that garden of mind."
> — Ch. II, closing.

> "They had left school wholly uneducated."
> — the "Twins," quoted in the Supplementary. The gravamen of Mason's whole critique of Public Schools in a single line.

## Open Questions & Gaps

> [!question] Mason contrasts Kerschensteiner's Munich continuation schools unfavourably with Grundtvig's Danish People's High Schools. How accurate is her characterisation of Kerschensteiner? She appears to collapse him with a generic "utilitarian" position and treats him as a foil rather than engaging his actual pedagogy of the _Arbeitsschule_. A future ingest of Kerschensteiner directly would test this.

> [!question] The "Twins" in the Supplementary are unnamed but identifiable as real historical figures (Eton old boys born in 1880, serving in sport, the City, Egypt under Cromer; both dead by ~1919). Who were they? Identifying them would make the Supplementary's indictment less literary and more pointed — or undermine it.

> [!question] Mason claims, of Council school pupils in poor districts, that "the work of the two is surprisingly equal" compared with privately-educated children. She cites no external inspector — the evidence is PNEU examination papers marked internally. Are there Gloucestershire Inspectorate records, HMI reports, or independent observers who corroborated this?

> [!question] The Ch. IV.IV passage on mediaeval unity of knowledge (Memmi's fresco, "all knowledge from above") is strikingly Ruskinian. Is Mason's philosophy of knowledge essentially Ruskin's _Mornings in Florence_ applied to pedagogy? If so, the Ruskin debts deserve tracing.

> [!question] Mason's rejection of Rousseau and of "self-expression" in the Ch. IV.V passage is pointed. But "Children are born persons" sounds Rousseauian on its face. Where exactly does Mason's anthropology diverge from Rousseau's — and does she engage _Emile_ beyond the passing dismissal here?

> [!question] The book treats mathematics as the one domain exempt from the "literary form" rule — "mathematics, like music, is a speech in itself." Yet Mason says little about how mathematics is actually taught in PUS. Book II leaves a gap on mathematical pedagogy that Book I did not fill.

> [!question] Mason never reckons with the cost (in teacher skill, parent book-buying, library access) of implementing her programme in a truly impoverished setting. The Council schools she cites had PNEU programmes because a committed HMI and a local advocate arranged it. Is her method scalable without that local advocacy?
