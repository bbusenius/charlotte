# Narration Before Composition

## Definition

In [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]]'s early-years language-arts method, children under nine are not taught formal composition as a separate exercise. Composition grows out of [[concepts/narration|narration]]: the child hears or reads living prose and tells it back. The written mechanics are trained separately through copywork, transcription, spelling, and dictation until written narration can begin later.

## Core Ideas

- **Narration is composition in oral form.** Mason treats telling back as the child's natural act of composition: selecting, sequencing, and shaping what has been read without a separate composition lesson.
- **Formal composition under nine is refused.** _Home Education_ calls set composition exercises for young children an educational mistake; the work of composition is already being done through narration.
- **Mechanical writing is trained separately.** Copywork, transcription, spelling, and dictation give the hand and eye their own practice without making the child invent content at the same time.
- **Style is caught from living prose.** The child forms language by hearing and reading good books, then reproducing and recomposing their substance through narration.
- **Written narration comes later.** Mason's later Form structure introduces written narration after the oral habit and mechanical foundations are in place.

## In Practice

For children under nine, the sequence is deliberately modest:

- The teacher reads from a living book.
- The child narrates orally.
- The child copies well-chosen text in copywork.
- Transcription and prepared dictation train spelling, attention, and written accuracy.
- Formal original composition is not assigned as its own lesson.

The practical result is a split between mental composition and mechanical writing: narration carries the child's thought and language; copywork and dictation carry the hand, eye, and spelling.
