# Nature Study

## Definition

In [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]]'s system, **nature study** is the young child's first and most important education: a first-hand, unhurried, out-of-door acquaintance with the actual things of the natural world — plants, trees, birds, insects, weather, landscape — gained through the child's own senses rather than through books or lessons. _Home Education_, Part II ("Out-of-Door Life for the Children"), is the wiki's source. Mason treats it not as a subject among others but as "the chief function of the child" in the first six or seven years.

## Core Ideas

- **The child's business is to find things out for himself.** "There is no sort of knowledge to be got in these early years so valuable to children as that which they get for themselves of the world they live in." The intellectual education of the young child "should lie in the free exercise of perceptive power."
- **A child learns from _things_, not words.** "Knowledge of things flies to the mind of a child as steel filings to a magnet." Words are "only the _signs_ of things"; the early years are "the storing time," to be spent "laying up images of things familiar" that later thought will draw on.
- **Long hours, out of doors, much of it let alone.** Mason urges four to six hours daily in the open from April to October, two or three in winter. Most of that time the child is [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|left to himself]] — "no story-books, no telling of tales, as little talk as possible." The mother's perpetual "cackle" leaves the child no room "to wonder—and grow."
- **Observation is trained, then recorded.** Through games of "sight-seeing" (who can see and truthfully tell the most about a cottage or hedge) and "picture-painting" (getting "a bit of landscape by heart" and calling up the mental image), the child's powers of observation, exact expression, and truthful statement are all trained at once.
- **The nature note-book and the calendar.** From age five or six the child keeps a **nature note-book** — a diary of what each day's walk shows him, freely illustrated with his own brush-drawings — and a **calendar** of first sightings (the first oak-leaf, the first tadpole, the first ripe blackberry), so that each year he knows when and where to look.
- **Reverence for life.** Mason refuses dissection and the destruction of living things for the young child; children are taught to **watch** patiently rather than to collect or kill. "Reverence for _life_... is a lesson of first importance."
- **Every natural object is "a member of a series."** First-hand observation of a pebble or an elder twig is the unforced beginning of real science: the child gathers "the common information which is the basis of scientific knowledge" long before he meets classification or nomenclature.
- **Geography begins out of doors.** A pond is a first lake, a hillock a first mountain; the child learns distance by pacing, direction by the sun and a pocket compass, and draws rough plans of fields — "pictorial geography" before any map.

## In Practice

- Daily walks, long and in (where possible) real country, with the mother present but mostly silent.
- "Sight-seeing" and "picture-painting" as games that double as lessons in observation and truthful description.
- A nature note-book kept by the child, with brush-drawings; a seasonal calendar of first sightings.
- Half a dozen named trees adopted as "yearlong friends"; wild flowers known by sight, habitat, and life-history.
- Living creatures watched in situ or in simple home set-ups (Mason gives instructions for a glass ant-nest); naturalists' books used to feed the child's curiosity, not replace his looking.
- The mother permits herself two interventions only, and rarely: to point out some special loveliness, and to name a flower or tree as "a beautiful _thought_ of God."

## Variants & Related Concepts

- [[concepts/science-of-relations|Science of Relations]] — nature study opens the child's relations with the natural world, one of the great fields of relation.
- [[concepts/masterly-inactivity|Masterly Inactivity]] — out-of-door life is the setting where "wise letting alone" is most clearly required.
- [[concepts/habit-of-attention|The Habit of Attention]] — patient watching of a bird or a "grub" is among the best trainings of attention.
- [[concepts/living-books|Living Books]] — naturalists' books (Mason names Kingsley, Gilbert White, Mrs Brightwen) feed curiosity but never substitute for first-hand looking.
- [[concepts/knowledge-as-food|Knowledge as the Food of the Mind]] — sense-knowledge of nature is "the intellectual food which the working faculty of his brain at this period requires."

**For the homeschool-hub skills:** nature study is the pedagogical grounding for nature-notebook page materials and for out-of-door / field-trip activities in early-years units.

## Key Proponents / Origins

- [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] — the developed early-years programme is hers. She draws on the Romantic naturalism of Wordsworth (quoted throughout Part II) and on the popular naturalist literature of her day (Gilbert White's _Selborne_, Kingsley, Audubon).
