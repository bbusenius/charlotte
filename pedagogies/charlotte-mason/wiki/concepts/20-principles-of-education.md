# 20 Principles of Education

## Definition

The numbered creed with which [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] opens [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|_Towards a Philosophy of Education_]] — a compact, roughly one-page statement of every doctrine the rest of the book elaborates. In the homeschool world the 20 Principles have since travelled as a freestanding document, often quoted without the book attached.

## Core Ideas

The list interlocks rather than enumerates. Its load-bearing claims (paraphrased, not quoted verbatim from the source):

1. **Children are born persons** — whole human beings, not blank slates. See [[concepts/children-are-born-persons|Children are Born Persons]].
2. Children are **not born either good or bad** but with possibilities for both.
3. The principles of **authority and obedience** are natural, necessary, and fundamental.
4. These principles are bounded: authority is limited by the personality of the child and by the specific task at hand. See [[concepts/education-is-atmosphere-discipline-life|atmosphere, discipline, life]] on the limits of adult action.
5. Three educational instruments are legitimate: **the atmosphere of environment, the discipline of habit, and the presentation of living ideas**. Anything else is suggestion, manipulation, or drill.
6. **"Education is an atmosphere, a discipline, a life."** See [[concepts/education-is-atmosphere-discipline-life|concept page]].
7. _Atmosphere_ means the real home and world — not a contrived child's world scaled down to juvenile taste.
8. _Discipline_ means the careful formation of habits, chiefly of thought.
9. _Life_ means the mind's daily ration of living ideas, not pre-digested facts.
10. The mind **feeds on ideas** — as the body on food; idea is to mind as food is to body.
11. Curriculum must therefore be **generous and wide** — drawn from many subjects and many authors.
12. **Education is the science of relations** — the child is born with potential relations to an enormous range of things, and schooling exists to open and preserve them. See [[concepts/science-of-relations|Science of Relations]].
13. The only adequate means to this end is a curriculum of **[[concepts/living-books|living books]]** — written by single authors with literary power and real knowledge.
14. The act of knowing requires the child to do the work: to attend, and then to **[[concepts/narration|narrate]]** what has been read.
15. A [[concepts/single-reading|single attentive reading]] is sufficient and is insisted upon.
16. Two guides are supplied to the moral and intellectual life of the child: **the [[concepts/way-of-the-will|Way of the Will]]** and the Way of the Reason.
17. The Way of the Will is the power of **choice and self-command**, cultivated not by direct effort of will but by turning the attention to another subject (the "diversion of the will").
18. The Way of the Reason is the power to **demonstrate logically** from a given premise — but reason will demonstrate _any_ premise the will entertains, so character depends upon what ideas the will admits.
19. Therefore a child must be fed on **sound initial ideas** so that reason has right material to reason from.
20. "In the things of the Spirit, as in the things of the mind and the body, we live by admiration, hope and love."

(Precise wording varies slightly across editions; the substance is as above.)

> [!note] **The principles were not always twenty.** The 1906 fifth edition of [[sources/home-education|_Home Education_]] — Volume I of the series, drawn from lectures Mason gave in 1885 — opens with a **synopsis of eighteen numbered points**, not twenty. The substance is recognisably the same creed: children are born persons; the three instruments (atmosphere, the discipline of habit, living ideas); the mind as a "spiritual organism" with an appetite for knowledge; education as the science of relations; the Way of the Will and the Way of the Reason. Three differences are worth recording. (1) "Education is an atmosphere / a discipline / a life" appears as **three separate points** (6, 7, 8), later compressed into one principle with elaborations. (2) The 1906 list is **more openly polemical against [[entities/johann-herbart|Herbart]]**, naming the "apperception mass" and the "doctrine of interest" directly (points 9, 11). (3) It ends on the unity of the intellectual and spiritual life (point 18), where the 1923 list ends on "we live by admiration, hope and love" (Principle 20). The creed thus grew and was re-ordered across the series; the count "20" belongs to the final form, not to Mason's thought as a whole.

## In Practice

The Principles are Mason's operational checklist, not an abstract philosophy. Every classroom decision in the [[entities/parents-union-school|Parents' Union School]] can be traced back to one of them: the insistence on a single reading (Principle 15), the refusal of twaddle (Principle 13), the use of habits rather than punishments (Principle 8), the breadth of curriculum across Forms (Principle 11), and the terminal examinations without revision (Principles 10 and 14 — if the mind has been fed, it retains).

## Key Proponents / Origins

- [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] — author. The Principles crystallised across her six-volume _Home Education Series_; an eighteen-point form heads Volume I ([[sources/home-education|_Home Education_]], 1906), and the twenty-point form printed in [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-1|_Philosophy of Education_]] (1923) is her final version.
