# N. F. S. Grundtvig

**Type:** person (Nikolai Frederik Severin Grundtvig, 1783–1872)

## Summary

Danish poet, pastor, historian, and educational reformer; the "Father of the People's High Schools" (_Folkehøjskoler_). In [[sources/towards-a-philosophy-of-education-book-2|Book II]] Ch. III [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] holds up Grundtvig and his Danish movement as the model England should follow for Continuation Schools, in pointed contrast to the utilitarian Munich Continuation Schools of Dr. Kerschensteiner.

## Key Contributions / Characteristics

- Founded the Danish People's High School movement; by 1845 the first schools Grundtvig had dreamed of were operating, and by 1903–4 more than 3,000 men and roughly as many women attended them annually.
- Believed in a **humanistic, literary, Christian, and national** education — "some acquaintance with popular literature, especially with the poetry and history of one's own country" — not a technical or vocational one.
- His pupils summarised his teaching in three sayings: "Spirit is might; Spirit reveals itself in spirit; Spirit works only in freedom." Mason quotes these approvingly.
- Conceded, pessimistically, that "all is not for all" — books were not for the people; and so his schools taught through lecture, supplied by men who "had their country's literature and history at their fingers' ends." Mason's point of departure: she disagrees. Her PUS work, she says, has shown that "all _is_ for all," that even working-class children read and narrate literary books directly.
- Credited (on the testimony of Swedish and Danish contemporaries Mason cites) with the quality of Danish agriculture — a "well-grounded humanistic training provides the surest basis for business capacity," not least in farming.
- His schools are residential winter schools for students aged roughly 18–25, a model Mason acknowledges is not directly transferable to England, whose continuation-age cohort (14–18) and manufacturing economy differ.
