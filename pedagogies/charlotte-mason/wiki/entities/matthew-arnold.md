# Matthew Arnold

**Type:** person (1822–1888); English poet, cultural critic, and Her Majesty's Inspector of Schools

## Summary

Victorian poet, essayist, and HMI. [[entities/charlotte-mason|Charlotte Mason]] invokes Arnold repeatedly — most structurally for his **threefold classification of knowledge** (Divinity, Humanities, Science), which she adopts as the spine of her own curriculum as [[concepts/knowledge-of-god-man-universe|Knowledge of God, of Man, and of the Universe]]. Arnold also supplies Mason with stylistic touchstones (the "grand manner" of the English Bible), cautionary quotations ("Think clear, feel deep, bear fruit well"), and an established Anglophone vocabulary for the case that _letters_ are the vehicle of culture against philistine and utilitarian forces.

## Key Contributions / Characteristics

- **Threefold classification of knowledge:** Matthew Arnold's division of all knowledge into Divinity, the Humanities, and the knowledge of the physical world (Science) is adopted by Mason as her curricular architecture. She cites it approvingly but adds her own twist: she wants all three kinds "classed under the head of Humanism" because all reach mind through literary form.
- Phrase-maker whose diction pervades _Philosophy of Education_ — "the grand manner" (of the English Bible), "that bent leather" (Scott anecdote), etc.
- As HMI, Arnold's career authorises Mason's own confidence in speaking about state elementary schools; his _Reports on Elementary Schools_ (1852–82) are the background Mason's Council-school claims stand in front of.
- Closing epigraph of Book II's "Knowledge in Literary Form" chapter: "Think clear, feel deep, bear fruit well."

## Connections

- Mason's [[concepts/knowledge-of-god-man-universe|threefold curriculum]] is directly descended from Arnold's classification
- His Ruskin/Arnold generation sets the Anglophone terms for Mason's "letters vs. utility" polemic
- The father, **Dr. Thomas Arnold** of Rugby, is cited separately by Mason as "that great remedial thinker" — on "very various reading" and on the cultivation of mental pictures from places described in books
